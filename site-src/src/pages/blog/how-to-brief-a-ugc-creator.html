why briefs (not calls) are what actually save you money

a rushed call feels efficient, but nothing gets written down, details drift between what you meant and what the creator heard, and by the time footage lands you're both guessing what "make it feel natural" was supposed to mean. a written brief forces you to decide the things that matter — the hook, the must-say claims, the shot list — before the shoot, not after. it also gives the creator something to work from independently, which is the whole point of ugc: real people directed like a small production, not a brand team hovering over every take.

what a good brief actually contains

the hook, written out word for word

don't describe the vibe of the hook — write the actual first line, or three options for the creator to choose between. the first 1–2 seconds decide whether anyone watches the rest, so this is the one section that shouldn't be left to improvisation. see 27 UGC hooks that stop the scroll (with scripts) for patterns that tend to work.

the one thing the video has to communicate

every ugc ad should be built around a single core message — the pain point, the payoff, or the offer. list every "must-say" claim explicitly, especially ones with legal or compliance weight (pricing, ingredients, results), and separate those from lines the creator can say in their own words.

shot list, not a script for everything

scripting every syllable produces stiff, obviously-scripted content, which defeats the point of ugc. instead, give a beat-by-beat shot list (problem, discovery, demo, payoff, cta) and let the creator fill in their own phrasing around the required claims.

tone and format references

one or two example videos — yours or a competitor's — communicate tone faster than a paragraph of adjectives. specify aspect ratio, platform (tiktok, reels, shorts, snap, pinterest), and rough length, since a 60-second youtube short and a 15-second tiktok need different pacing.

do's and don'ts

the fastest way to prevent reshoots: a short, specific list of things not to do (no visible competitor logos, no unapproved claims, no background music with lyrics) sitting right next to the shot list, not buried in a separate document nobody opens.

deliverables and usage

state exactly what files you need (raw clips, edited cut, both), how many variations, and what usage rights or whitelisting access is included in the fee — see Spark Ads, whitelisting and usage rights explained for the detail on tiktok's spark ads and meta's partnership ads.

pre-shoot checklist

run through this before the creator presses record, not after:

  • [ ] hook is written out, not just described
  • [ ] the single core message is one sentence, not a list of five things
  • [ ] must-say claims are separated from open-ended lines
  • [ ] shot list covers problem, demo, payoff and cta as distinct beats
  • [ ] platform and aspect ratio are confirmed (vertical 9:16 for most of these)
  • [ ] tone reference video is linked, not just described in adjectives
  • [ ] do's and don'ts list is specific (named competitors, banned claims, no visible logos)
  • [ ] product/sample has actually been shipped or confirmed in the creator's hands
  • [ ] usage rights and platform authorisation (spark ads code, partnership ad invite) are agreed and dated
  • [ ] deadline for draft and final cut is confirmed in writing
  • [ ] payment terms and revision rounds are agreed before the shoot, not after

common brief mistakes that cause reshoots

vague hooks. "start with something attention-grabbing" isn't a hook, it's a wish. write actual lines.

scripting every word. the opposite failure — a fully scripted video reads as an ad, not ugc, and the read-back plateaus engagement rather than lifting it.

no do's and don'ts. without a short banned-list, creators guess, and guesses are what get reshot.

missing usage terms. if the brief doesn't say whether the fee includes whitelisting access, that conversation happens after the footage lands, which is the most expensive time to have it — covered in full in Spark Ads, whitelisting and usage rights explained.

no reference video. tone described only in adjectives ("fun, authentic, punchy") gets interpreted five different ways by five different creators.

faq

how long should a ugc brief be?
one to two pages is normal. long enough to cover the hook, shot list, must-say claims, do's and don'ts, and deliverables — short enough that a creator actually reads the whole thing before filming.

should i write a full script or just a shot list?
a shot list with a written hook and must-say claims, not a full script. scripting every line makes the video feel like an ad rather than a genuine recommendation, which tends to hurt performance rather than help it.

what's the single biggest cause of reshoots?
a hook that was described rather than written out, and a missing do's and don'ts list — both are five-minute fixes at the brief stage and expensive to fix once footage is in.

do i need a different brief for each platform?
the core message and shot list can usually stay the same; adjust aspect ratio, length and pacing per platform, since a tiktok hook needs to land faster than a longer-form youtube short.
