a hook is the first 1–3 seconds of an ad — the line or moment that decides whether someone keeps watching or scrolls past. it matters more than the product shot, the edit or the caption, because none of those get seen if the hook fails. below are 27 tiktok and reels ad hooks grouped by type, each with a one-line script you can adapt and a note on when to reach for it.

why the hook matters more than the product shot

you can have the best product in the category and a beautifully lit video, and it still won't work if the first line doesn't earn the next three seconds. most ugc briefs should spend as much time on the hook as on everything else combined — it's also the cheapest thing to test, since one piece of footage can carry five different hooks without a reshoot. for more on how to plan that testing, see How to brief a UGC creator: template and checklist.

the 27 hooks, grouped by type

curiosity hooks

these open a loop the viewer wants closed. they work best when there's a genuinely surprising mechanism or result behind the product — don't force curiosity onto something ordinary, it just reads as clickbait.

  1. the "nobody tells you this" hook — "the thing nobody tells you about dry skin isn't the product, it's this." use when: your product solves a root cause people don't know about, e.g. a skincare serum.
  2. the unfinished sentence — "i wasn't going to post this but..." use when: you want the video to feel unscripted and leaked rather than advertised.
  3. the number tease — "there are 3 ingredients in this that i didn't expect." use when: your product has a genuinely specific, checkable detail.
  4. the "wait for it" hook — "watch what happens when i pour this next to a normal one." use when: there's a visual payoff later in the video worth waiting for.
  5. the confession opener — "i've been lying to my followers about my skincare routine." use when: you want a scroll-stopping personal angle before the reveal.

problem-first hooks

lead with the pain, not the product. these work because the viewer self-selects — if the problem lands, they're already invested before they know what you're selling.

  1. the direct callout — "if your oat milk always splits in coffee, this is for you." use when: your audience has a specific, nameable frustration.
  2. the relatable rant — "i am SO tired of activewear that pills after two washes." use when: the category has a common complaint your product actually fixes.
  3. the "why does this still happen" hook — "why is it 2026 and my plants are still dying?" use when: the problem feels absurdly unsolved.
  4. the cost-of-inaction hook — "this is what happens if you keep ignoring your gut health." use when: the consequence of not acting is visual or dramatic.
  5. the mistake reveal — "i was doing this completely wrong for years." use when: you can position the product as the fix for a common mistake, not just a nice-to-have.

social proof hooks

these borrow trust from other people — reviews, sales numbers, or a wave of adoption — rather than the creator's own opinion.

  1. the sold-out hook — "this sold out three times before i finally got one." use when: there's real demand you can point to, framed as typical, not invented.
  2. the review-reaction hook — "the reviews on this made me buy it in five minutes." use when: your product has strong, verifiable reviews worth reacting to on camera.
  3. the everyone's-doing-it hook — "why is everyone suddenly switching to this?" use when: there's a visible trend or shift you can point to honestly.
  4. the friend recommendation hook — "my friend wouldn't stop talking about this so i tried it." use when: the format is a casual, low-pressure recommendation rather than a hard sell.

pattern interrupt hooks

these break the scroll through something visually or tonally unexpected — a jump cut, an odd first frame, or a line that doesn't sound like an ad.

  1. the in-media-res hook — open mid-action, already holding the product, no intro at all. use when: your platform's algorithm rewards videos that don't look ad-like in the first frame.
  2. the wrong-question hook — "how many of these can i fit in my bag?" use when: you want a playful, off-kilter angle instead of a straight pitch.
  3. the loud-noise or visual jolt — start on an unexpected close-up or sound (a fizz, a snap, a slam) before speaking. use when: the product has a satisfying physical or audio quality, e.g. the crack of a can.
  4. the fake-out hook — "this is not a sponsored video... okay it kind of is." use when: you want to acknowledge the ad format itself for a laugh, then keep going.
  5. the silent caption hook — no spoken opener, just bold on-screen text over unexpected footage. use when: sound-off viewing is high on your platform and the visual alone needs to stop the thumb.

pov hooks

these put the viewer in a specific moment or role, borrowing tiktok's native "pov:" format. they work because they feel like a scene, not an ad.

  1. the pov: format — "pov: you finally found leggings that don't roll down." use when: the product solves something visceral and physical.
  2. the day-in-the-life hook — "6am, and this is the only thing getting me out of bed." use when: the product fits naturally into a routine you can show start to finish.
  3. the before-you-hook — "before you leave the house without doing this..." use when: you want to frame the product as a step people are currently skipping.
  4. the get-ready-with-me hook — "grwm but i'm explaining why i switched my whole skincare shelf." use when: the format is beauty, wellness or self-care and a routine context helps.

comparison hooks

these work by contrast — old vs new, cheap vs better, this brand vs that one — and are especially strong for products replacing a habit.

  1. the old-way-new-way hook — "how i used to make coffee vs how i make it now." use when: your product replaces an existing habit rather than creating a new one.
  2. the side-by-side hook — "£40 serum vs the £14 one, same ingredients list." use when: you can make an honest, specific comparison rather than a vague "better than."
  3. the expectation-vs-reality hook — "what i thought would happen vs what actually happened." use when: the real result is more impressive (or more surprising) than the assumption.
  4. the swap challenge hook — "i replaced my whole plant care kit for a month, here's what changed." use when: you have (or can genuinely run) a time-bound trial worth showing.

how to brief creators on hooks

a hook only works if the creator delivers it like a real thought, not a read-aloud script. when you brief one:

  • give the exact opening line, not just a vibe — creators improvise better from a strong first line than from "be natural"
  • brief 2–3 hooks per shoot so you get testable variations from one booking, not one bet
  • tell them what NOT to do in the first three seconds (no logo reveal, no "hey guys," no slow zoom-in) — most weak hooks fail because of pacing, not the idea itself
  • pair the hook with the platform: a pattern-interrupt hook earns more on tiktok's for-you page, while a pov hook tends to fit reels' more polished feed

faq

how long should a ugc hook be?
1–3 seconds of screen time, ideally within the first spoken line or on-screen text. if it takes longer than that to get to the point, it's not a hook.

do i need a different hook for tiktok vs reels vs shorts?
not always a different hook entirely, but a different edit of it — tiktok rewards rawer, faster pattern interrupts, while reels and shorts audiences tolerate a slightly more polished open. see UGC for ecommerce brands: the 30-day content engine for platform-by-platform notes.

how many hooks should i test per video?
3–5 per concept is a sensible starting point — enough to find a clear winner without paying for a full reshoot of everything. more on typical pricing for hook bundles in How much do UGC ads cost? 2026 rates and packages.

can one hook work for every product?
no — a curiosity hook needs something genuinely surprising to reveal, and a problem-first hook needs a real, specific pain point. pick the type based on what's actually true about your product, not what looks good on a hook list.
