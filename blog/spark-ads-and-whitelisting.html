spark ads, whitelisting and usage rights: the ugc legal bits explained

spark ads, whitelisting and usage rights explained: how tiktok and meta authorisation works, typical windows and what your creator agreement needs to say.

6 min readupdated 2026-09-06by the hypjam team

spark ads let you boost a creator's own tiktok post from their handle. whitelisting, meta's partnership ads, let you run ads through a creator's instagram or facebook profile. usage rights are the separate permission to reuse the raw clip elsewhere. mix the three up and you'll spend week one chasing re-approvals instead of spending budget. here's what each term means, how authorisation works on both platforms, typical windows, and what belongs in a creator agreement. (general information, not legal advice.)

three different things, one confusing vocabulary

everyone in ugc land throws "whitelisting" around as a catch-all, but it's worth splitting the concept into three separate mechanics, because each one is unlocked differently and covers a different use case.

spark ads (tiktok)

spark ads is tiktok's own feature for running paid ads off an existing organic post while keeping the creator's handle, profile picture, likes, comments and share count attached. viewers see it as a creator's post that happens to be boosted, not a brand ad dressed up as one — which is typically why spark ads outperforms a cold upload of the identical video from a brand handle.

whitelisting / partnership ads (meta)

"whitelisting" is creator-economy slang left over from the earlier version of this feature. meta's actual product is partnership ads (previously called branded content ads). the mechanic: a creator tags your brand as a paid partner on an instagram post, then grants your ad account permission to run ads through their profile. the ad displays "paid partnership with [brand]" and runs with the creator's identity, but you control targeting, budget and placement from your own ads manager.

usage rights / creator licensing

this is the one platform toggles don't cover. usage rights are the licence you negotiate in the contract to use the raw footage — the actual mp4 file — anywhere whitelisting doesn't reach: ads posted from your own brand handle, landing pages, email, paid social on channels the creator isn't tagged into, retail screens, decks. no button grants this. it lives entirely in your agreement, which is why point four below matters more than the platform mechanics.

how authorisation works on tiktok

  1. the creator posts (or you post from their logged-in account) the organic tiktok video.
  2. in the tiktok app, the creator goes to settings and privacy, then ad settings, then ad authorization, and generates a code tied to that specific video.
  3. when generating the code, the creator sets a duration — tiktok's default is 30 days, but it can be extended up to 365 days.
  4. the creator shares that code with you or your agency.
  5. you enter the code in tiktok ads manager when building the spark ads campaign, and the ad starts running off the creator's post.

when the code expires, delivery stops — the ad doesn't quietly switch to running as your brand. build renewal reminders into your workflow, especially for always-on or evergreen creators, so a campaign never pauses because a code lapsed two weeks into flight.

how authorisation works on meta

  1. your ad account sends the creator a partnership ad invite through meta business suite (or the creator initiates by tagging your brand as a paid partner on their post).
  2. the creator opens the branded content settings on their instagram post and accepts, ticking the option that allows the ad partner to run ads through it.
  3. once accepted, your team can build ads in ads manager using the creator's profile as the ad's identity — their name and photo run the ad, labelled as a paid partnership.
  4. unlike tiktok's code, meta doesn't force an expiry date on this access. it persists until the creator revokes it or you remove them from the campaign.

because meta doesn't auto-expire access, set your own window in the agreement and build a step into offboarding where the creator revokes partnership status once the window ends. don't rely on the platform to enforce a deadline nobody wrote down.

typical usage windows

these are common starting points from agency practice, not fixed rules — always confirm the actual terms in your own agreement.

  • organic-only usage (creator posts, brand doesn't touch ads manager): often unrestricted or capped at 12 months, since there's no paid spend riding on it.
  • spark ads / partnership ads access: 30, 60 or 90 days is a typical opening ask for a single-campaign brief; evergreen or retainer creators sometimes grant 6–12 months with a renewal check-in.
  • full usage rights buyout (raw footage used off-platform, on your own handles, in other creators' content, in paid social beyond the tagged post): commonly 3, 6 or 12 months, with a separate renewal fee if you want to keep running it past that.
  • perpetual or "in perpetuity" rights: possible, but it's a different price point — treat it as a distinct line item, not something that's implied by a standard usage fee.

whatever you land on, put the exact number of days and the exact platforms in writing. "usage rights included" without a duration or a platform list is the single most common source of billing disputes and awkward mid-campaign renegotiations.

what to put in a creator agreement (not legal advice)

a proper creator agreement earns its keep by making renewal, disputes and takedowns boring instead of stressful. at minimum, get these in writing:

  • scope of usage — organic only, paid whitelisting/partnership ads, full off-platform buyout, or a combination, spelled out separately.
  • named platforms — tiktok, instagram, facebook, youtube shorts, pinterest, snap — list them; "social media" is not specific enough.
  • duration — start date, end date or number of days, and what happens on expiry (auto-off, or renewal at an agreed fee).
  • authorisation mechanics — who generates the spark ads code or accepts the partnership ad invite, and by what date relative to the shoot or launch.
  • exclusivity — whether the creator is barred from working with competing brands in the category for a period, and how "competing" is defined.
  • compensation structure — flat usage fee, cpm-based whitelisting fee, or a hybrid, and how renewal is priced if you extend.
  • ownership vs licence — most ugc agreements licence the footage rather than transfer copyright; state which one you're doing.
  • early revocation — notice period the creator must give if they want to pull access before the window ends.
  • content labelling — confirmation the creator will use the platform's required paid-partnership or ad disclosure tags.
  • admin access details — who holds login or manager access to run the ads, and when that access is removed at the end.
  • revisions and deliverables — separate from usage rights, but worth bundling into the same document so there's one source of truth.

none of this is legal advice — templates are a starting point, not a substitute for a lawyer reviewing your specific deal, especially once real budget or exclusivity is on the table. for the brief that feeds into this agreement, see how to brief a ugc creator so the first cut is the final cut.

faq

is whitelisting the same thing as spark ads? not quite. spark ads is tiktok's specific mechanic. whitelisting is the informal, cross-platform term people use loosely; on meta the actual feature is called partnership ads. same idea — running ads through a creator's handle — different platform, different buttons.

do i need a separate usage rights clause for each platform? yes, ideally. name the platforms explicitly in the agreement rather than writing "social media," since tiktok, meta and youtube each have their own authorisation mechanics and none of them cover off-platform use automatically.

what happens if a creator revokes access mid-campaign? on tiktok, the ad stops delivering once the authorisation code expires or is revoked. on meta, access can be pulled by the creator at any point, which is why your agreement should require a notice period before they do.

can i use ugc footage in my own ads without whitelisting? yes — if your agreement includes usage rights for the raw footage, you can upload it as an ad from your own brand handle. whitelisting/spark ads and usage rights are separate permissions and you can have one without the other.
